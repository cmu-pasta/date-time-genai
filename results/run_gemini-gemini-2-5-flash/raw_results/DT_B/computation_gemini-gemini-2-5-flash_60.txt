Let's think step by step to solve this problem.

1.  **Understand Solar Noon**: Solar noon is the moment when the Sun crosses the local celestial meridian and reaches its highest position in the sky at a given location. This is often *not* exactly 12:00 PM on a clock due to several factors, primarily the Equation of Time and the observer's longitude relative to their time zone's standard meridian.

2.  **Limitations with `datetime` only**: The `datetime` library in Python is excellent for date and time manipulation, but it does not inherently contain astronomical algorithms needed to precisely calculate the Equation of Time or the sun's position in the sky. To perform a truly accurate solar noon calculation, external libraries (like `pyephem` or `skyfield`) that implement ephemeris calculations would be necessary.

3.  **Approximation Strategy (within `datetime` limits)**: Given the strict constraint to use *only* the `datetime` library, we must make an approximation. A common approximation for solar noon considers the longitude. The Earth rotates 15 degrees per hour, meaning each degree of longitude corresponds to a 4-minute shift in the local apparent time.
    *   We will assume a 'mean' solar noon, ignoring the Equation of Time (which can shift solar noon by up to ~16 minutes from the mean).
    *   We will calculate the difference in time based on the given longitude relative to the Greenwich Meridian (0° longitude) or a conceptual standard meridian.
    *   If we assume that local *mean* solar noon occurs at 12:00 PM local time *if the observer is on the standard meridian of their time zone*, then we need to adjust for the actual longitude.
    *   For simplicity and to stick to `datetime` objects, we will calculate the time when the sun is at its highest point *relative to UTC 12:00 PM* at 0 degrees longitude, and then adjust for the given longitude.

4.  **Detailed Steps for Approximation**:
    *   **Input**: A `datetime.date` object (for the specific day) and two `float` values for latitude and longitude. The latitude is not strictly needed for this simplified longitude-based calculation but is part of the problem statement for location coordinates.
    *   **Base Time**: Start with 12:00 PM UTC on the given date.
    *   **Longitude Adjustment**: Calculate the time shift. Each degree of longitude corresponds to 4 minutes (60 minutes / 15 degrees per hour). East longitudes are "ahead" (earlier UTC time for solar noon), and West longitudes are "behind" (later UTC time for solar noon).
        *   `time_shift_minutes = longitude * 4`
        *   Convert this to a `datetime.timedelta`.
    *   **Calculate Solar Noon UTC**: Add this `timedelta` to the base 12:00 PM UTC.
    *   **Return Type**: The problem asks for a `datetime` object. The returned object will represent the solar noon time in UTC. If a local time is desired, further timezone conversion would be needed, but the output type constraint is `datetime`.

```python
from datetime import datetime, date, timedelta, time, timezone

def calculate_solar_noon_time(d: date, latitude: float, longitude: float) -> datetime:
    """
    Calculates an approximate solar noon time for a given date and location.
    This approximation primarily accounts for longitude and ignores the Equation of Time,
    which requires astronomical calculations beyond the scope of the standard datetime library.
    The result is returned in UTC.

    Args:
        d (date): The date for which to calculate solar noon.
        latitude (float): The latitude of the location (degrees). Not used in this simplified
                          calculation but included as per problem statement.
        longitude (float): The longitude of the location (degrees, positive for East, negative for West).

    Returns:
        datetime: The approximate solar noon time in UTC for the given date and longitude.
                  This datetime object will be timezone-aware (UTC).
    """
    # Step 1: Create a base UTC datetime object for noon on the given date.
    # We assume mean solar noon is roughly 12:00 PM UTC at the Prime Meridian (0 longitude).
    base_utc_noon = datetime.combine(d, time(12, 0, 0), tzinfo=timezone.utc)

    # Step 2: Calculate the time adjustment due to longitude.
    # The Earth rotates 15 degrees per hour, so 1 degree = 4 minutes.
    # Positive longitude (East) means solar noon occurs earlier in UTC.
    # Negative longitude (West) means solar noon occurs later in UTC.
    # So, we subtract for East longitude and add for West longitude.
    # Or simply: subtract (longitude * 4 minutes) from the base UTC noon.
    longitude_offset_minutes = longitude * 4
    
    # Create a timedelta for the longitude offset
    # A negative offset for positive longitude means it happens earlier UTC.
    # A positive offset for negative longitude means it happens later UTC.
    time_adjustment = timedelta(minutes=-longitude_offset_minutes)

    # Step 3: Apply the adjustment to find the approximate solar noon.
    solar_noon_utc = base_utc_noon + time_adjustment
    
    # Step 4: Return the result as a timezone-aware datetime object (UTC).
    return solar_noon_utc
```
